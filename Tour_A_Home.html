Tour With a Plan, Not Just a Vibe

A great home tour isn't about deciding whether you can picture yourself living there in the first two minutes. It's about learning how the home functions, what condition it's in, what may cost money later, and whether it works for your real life. Start inside. Move through the house calmly and systematically — don't rush, and don't "move in" emotionally before you understand the property.

Quick Reference

Tour in This Order

  1. Pause outside and look at the property as a whole
  2. Tour the interior with a calm, consistent process
  3. Review utility and mechanical areas
  4. Walk the exterior, lot, driveway, and yard
  5. Write down your observations before you leave

The goal isn't to decide whether the house is "perfect." The goal is to understand whether it works for you, what needs verification, and whether it deserves another look.

Step 1

Come Prepared and Pause

Before you head to a showing, come prepared with:

  • ✓Your phone with a notes app and camera
  • ✓A tape measure or laser measure
  • ✓A small flashlight
  • ✓Practical shoes — for the yard, basement, attic access, and uneven ground
  • ✓A copy of your must-haves and deal-breakers
  • ✓A simple scorecard for each house

Before walking in, pause at the street for 60 seconds. Look at the house as a whole. Consider your first impressions and note any concerns.

Step 2

Start Inside

During a tour it's reasonable to observe visible condition, open and close doors, operate normal light switches, and test fixtures when permitted. Focus on room layout and flow, general condition and upkeep, water stains or damage, system age, and neighborhood fit. Do not focus on staging or contents — they will be gone if the home becomes yours.

1. Check Whether the Layout Works

Ask yourself practical questions, not emotional ones:

  • Does the entry work for daily life — guests, shoes, bags, pets, deliveries?
  • Is there enough usable living space, not just enough square footage?
  • Does the kitchen connect well to dining, living, outdoor space, and storage?
  • Can your actual furniture fit where it needs to go?
  • Are bedrooms private enough and large enough?
  • Is there a workable home-office, playroom, hobby, guest, or flex-space solution?
  • Are laundry and storage where you need them?
  • Does the home work for your household now — and for the next several years?
  • Are stairs, narrow hallways, low ceilings, or bathroom layouts a concern for your needs?

2. Look Up, Down, and Around

In every room, take a slow visual sweep.

Ceilings & walls

  • Water stains, discoloration, bubbling or peeling paint, patched areas
  • Cracks that are wide, long, diagonal, repeated, or visible around doors and windows
  • Sagging ceilings or visible drywall seams
  • Mold-like spotting, mildew, or unexplained odors

Floors, doors & windows

  • Sloping, soft, bouncy, uneven, or squeaky floors
  • Gaps, stains, buckling, warped flooring, or damaged tile
  • Carpet stains or odors that may be hiding pet, moisture, or smoke issues
  • Doors that stick, rub, won't latch, or swing on their own
  • Windows that appear fogged between panes, damaged, or poorly sealed
  • Cracked trim or gaps around window and door frames

3. Kitchen & Bathrooms

These rooms often reveal how a home has actually been maintained.

Kitchen checklist

  • Enough cabinet, pantry, counter, and refrigerator space?
  • Drawers and cabinet doors open smoothly?
  • Visible water damage beneath the sink?
  • Appliances included, and do they appear maintained?
  • Adequate lighting and outlet placement?
  • Is the range vented outside, recirculating, or absent?

Bathroom checklist

  • Run water briefly, if allowed — note pressure, drainage, leaks, unusual sounds
  • Check under sinks for moisture, corrosion, stains, or repairs
  • Loose toilets, cracked tile, failing caulk, or damaged grout
  • Humidity or mold concerns near ceilings, exhaust fans, and windows
  • Does the bathroom count, location, storage, and layout work for your household?
Step 3

Review Utility & Mechanical Areas

You're not performing an inspection — you're looking for visible clues that justify further questions. Don't let a nicely staged living room distract you from the mechanical areas; systems aren't as exciting as countertops, but they matter far more to the cost of ownership.

Check

  • HVAC equipment — apparent age, rust, leaking, odd noises, neglected filters
  • Electrical panel — general condition, labeling, corrosion, exposed wiring
  • Water heater — apparent age, corrosion, pan/drain setup, surrounding moisture
  • Washer & dryer connections — leaks, venting, drainage, location
  • Basement, crawlspace & attic access — damp smells, stains, standing water, pests
  • Smoke and carbon-monoxide detectors — visible presence and reasonable placement
  • Fireplace or wood stove — condition, ventilation, chimney, decorative or functional

Redfin's own buyer touring guidance similarly recommends paying close attention to visible structural issues, uneven floors, water damage, mold or mildew signs, roof and siding condition, and major system concerns.

Step 4

Walk the Exterior, Lot & Yard

After you understand the inside, step outside and evaluate the house as a building on a piece of land.

Roof, gutters & drainage

  • Missing, curling, damaged, or uneven roof shingles
  • Sagging rooflines or visible patchwork
  • Gutters that are loose, clogged, missing, or poorly directed
  • Downspouts that empty close to the foundation
  • Erosion, standing water, or ground that slopes toward the house
  • Stains on siding, soffits, fascia, or exterior walls
  • Trees or limbs hanging over the roof

Foundation & exterior structure

  • Large, repeated, horizontal, stair-step, or widening cracks
  • Water stains or efflorescence on masonry
  • Areas where siding, brick, trim, or stucco appear damaged or poorly repaired
  • Rotting wood, peeling paint, deteriorated caulk, or insect damage
  • Gaps around pipes, vents, windows, or siding transitions
  • Loose railings, unsafe steps, damaged decks, or aging porches

Yard & lot

  • Grading and drainage after rain
  • Fencing condition, privacy, and lot boundaries — including visible encroachments, access points, and shared-driveway questions
  • Driveway width, condition, parking capacity, turnaround space, and slope
  • Retaining walls, sheds, pools, decks, patios, and outbuildings
  • Mature trees that add beauty but also future maintenance
  • Sun exposure, shade, wind, garden space, and usable outdoor area
  • Noise from roads, neighbors, rail lines, aircraft, businesses, or recreation areas
  • Adjacent vacant land, large tracts, or commercial parcels that may develop later
  • Whether the outdoor space actually works for pets, children, entertaining, gardening, or your hobbies
Words of Caution⚠

What Not To Do

Don't "move in" too early

Staging is designed to help a home feel attractive. Let it do that — but don't let it become the whole story. Avoid spending the first ten minutes planning where the couch will go. First determine: does the home meet our practical needs? Is the visible condition acceptable? What needs a closer look? What could become a meaningful cost? What information is missing? Does the location work for our routine? Only after those questions are answered should the emotional "Can I see myself living here?" question lead the decision.

Don't confuse cosmetics with condition

Fresh paint, trendy fixtures, staged furniture, and new flooring can make a home feel polished. They don't answer questions about drainage, roof life, HVAC age, wiring, plumbing, foundation movement, or layout limitations.

Don't over-test the home

Be respectful of the seller's property and the showing rules. Don't open locked areas, climb onto roofs, move furniture, operate unfamiliar equipment, enter crawlspaces, or conduct an informal "inspection." If something needs deeper evaluation, note it for a qualified professional.

Don't bring extra guests or pets

Don't bring extra guests or animals without clearing it with your agent first. Pets generally should be left at home — showings move fast, and an unexpected crowd or an anxious pet can complicate access, safety, and the seller's own belongings or animals.

Reference

Questions to Ask During or After a Tour

  • How old are the roof, HVAC, water heater, windows, and major appliances?
  • What repairs, replacements, or renovations have been completed recently?
  • Have there been water, drainage, foundation, plumbing, electrical, mold, pest, or insurance issues?
  • What utilities serve the property, and what are typical costs?
  • Is there an HOA, and what are the fees, restrictions, and pending projects?
  • Are there shared driveways, easements, septic systems, wells, flood concerns, or maintenance agreements?
  • What conveys with the property?
  • How long has the home been on the market, and has it been under contract before?
  • Are there disclosures, permits, surveys, inspection reports, or repair records available?

Step 5

Write Down Your Observations

Before driving away, rate the home while it's fresh in your mind — it prevents the "all the houses are running together" problem and helps you compare homes on substance rather than memory.

Then write:

  • My top three positives
  • My top three concerns
  • Questions that need answers
  • Repairs, updates, or costs to investigate
  • Would I tour again, pursue, compare, or eliminate?
Pro Tip Fill out the scorecard before you leave the driveway — memory fades fast once you're touring back-to-back homes, and a home you loved at 10am can blur with one you saw at 2pm.
