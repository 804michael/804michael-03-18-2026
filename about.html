Where It Started

Raised on Real Estate

I grew up in Annandale, in Fairfax County, helping my father maintain our family's rental properties. Some families talk about the weather or football at dinner. Mine talked about real estate. My grandfather started the brokerage I work under today, now called Malcolm Real Estate, and it has stayed in the family ever since. It is a small, family-only boutique firm. We help sellers and buyers with residential homes, and we have built, sold and still manage our own rental portfolio.

I earned my real estate license at 19. The exam felt easy, and I didn't understand why until people my age started joining my study groups and asking how I already knew this stuff. I had been absorbing it at the kitchen table for years. That was my first taste of teaching real estate, and I still enjoy taking something complicated and making it simple enough that anyone can follow it.

Why It Matters to You

What Fairfax County Taught Me About Richmond's Future

Growing up in Fairfax County in the 1980s and 90s meant watching a suburb fill in. Every open piece of land eventually got built on. Pinecrest Golf Course, near Route 236 and Braddock Road, became a large townhome development. Cameron Station, the Army base my father had worked on, was converted into townhomes and other high-density housing. Tysons Corner went from a crossroads to a major business and shopping center while I was in middle and high school.

By the time I finished college, land in close-in Fairfax was so valuable that 1,200 to 1,400 square foot homes from the 1940s and 50s, the three-bedroom, one-and-a-half-bath houses that had been starter homes, were being torn down. Houses of 3,000 to 4,000 square feet went up on the same lots. Places people once thought of as too far out became "close in."

Urban growth follows patterns, and the Richmond area is walking the same path a few decades behind. Here is how I see it lining up:

Years ago, while I was working in local government mental health, I had lunch with Hanover County's county administrator and mentioned that Hanover felt a lot like Northern Virginia. He laughed and told me the county had been consulting with Loudoun County, just west of Fairfax, on how to manage growth, and was learning from the lessons Loudoun learned the hard way.

Knowing where growth is heading helps you avoid a home that will be hard to sell later, and spot the areas likely to gain value first.

The Long Way Around

A Detour That Became an Advantage

Real estate wasn't a straight line for me. I avoided the family business for years, partly because I remembered all the nights and weekends it took when I was a kid.

  1. Architecture

    Earned a degree in Architecture from Virginia Tech, which is still how I look at a house: layout, flow, construction and how a floor plan will age.

  2. About 10 years in marketing, IT and design

    One job that combined marketing, IT and graphic design, with a lot of travel. It taught me how to connect with people and figure out what they actually want, which is not always what they first ask for.

  3. 2007: counseling

    Went back to school at VCU for a Master's in Rehabilitation Counseling and practiced for about a decade as a Licensed Professional Counselor, including work in local government mental health. I still hold my counseling license.

  4. 2012: back to real estate

    At a transition point in my counseling career, I opened a private counseling office and a real estate office side by side and gave both a year. Real estate won, because I enjoyed it far more. Getting relicensed was harder the second time, because I knew too much and kept overthinking the test questions. That led me to write short study primers that help others pass the real estate exam and other licensing tests by following a few simple rules.

  5. 2013 onward: full time

    Went out on my own in real estate full time and haven't looked back.

The counseling years matter more than people expect. Selling or buying a home is one of the most emotional things most people ever do, and one of the biggest reasons to hire an agent is emotional distance. I can stay calm and steady when a negotiation gets heated, and I can help a client think clearly when they are too close to the decision.

The Differentiator

The Broker Agents Called When a Deal Went Sideways

After a 20-year gap, I wanted to know exactly how real estate had changed. Virginia required 16 hours of continuing education for my first license renewal. I completed more than 100, because I basically lived in the classroom for my first year and a half.

I spent my first two to three years back at RE/MAX, learning the local contracts from excellent brokers. Too often, though, the answer to "why do we do it this way?" was "that's just how we do it." That wasn't enough for me. I went back to the original sources: the Code of Virginia, the regulations published by the Virginia Real Estate Board, and guidance from Virginia REALTORS and the National Association of REALTORS.

In 2015 I moved to Keller Williams, where my contract knowledge got noticed quickly. I held a broker's license, and I became the office's informal broker support for more than 200 agents, essentially a supervising broker without the formal title. That meant:

  • TeachingClasses on the Virginia residential purchase agreement, how to modify it correctly, and the addenda used for special situations.
  • ReviewingChecking the changes agents made to standard forms so their clients were actually covered.
  • RescuingBeing the call when a deal went sideways, for brand-new agents and top producers alike, and smoothing things over when clients got upset.
  • MediatingSettling disputes between agents over how a contract should be handled. Sometimes that meant telling a very experienced agent that the newer agent was right, because that is what the contract says.

My test for every transaction is simple. If this ended up in front of a judge, what does the paper say? It comes down to what is written in black and white, not how anyone feels about it. Many agents get emotionally attached to their deals. I stay calm and stick to the documents, and that keeps my clients out of trouble.

I know most of the ways a contract can go wrong. I use that to protect sellers from inexperienced agents on the other side of the deal, and to protect buyers from their own blind spots.

Hard-Won Lessons

What Losing Rental Homes Taught Me

During my years away from the business, I invested on my own and bought about a dozen homes in Orlando, Florida. I got overextended, carrying about $1.5 million in loans when the 2008 housing crash hit, and I lost six or seven of those homes to foreclosure.

It was a painful way to learn, and it shapes how I advise clients today. It is easy to buy real estate and much harder to sell it. Your money is really made on the purchase, so you have to buy right. It also means I don't judge anyone who has made a financial mistake or is facing a hard situation with a property. I have been there, and I know how to help people through it.

How I Work

Precision Over Volume

My goal is not to sell 100 or 500 homes a year. I would rather be precise and protect the people I work with, because clients who were well protected are the ones who send their friends and family to me later.

1

From the Contract Table

A Few Stories Worth Telling

A reverse reconsideration of value

$39,000 saved in a hot seller's market

In the 2021 to 2022 market, when most buyers were paying well over asking, I studied the tax records and the sale history on a home and knew it was priced above what the market supported. We negotiated $30,000 off the list price and still wrote an offer strong enough to be accepted.

Then the appraisal came back exactly at the contract price. When an appraisal comes in low, a buyer's agent usually asks for a reconsideration of value with better comparable sales to push the value up. I did the opposite. I submitted comparable sales that supported a lower value, and the price came down another $9,000. In a heavy seller's market, the seller ended up bringing money to closing.

And a couple of things clients have said to me that I think explain my approach better than I can:

  • "I feel like I have a PhD in real estate after spending all this time with you, and it wasn't that hard to understand."A buyer, after finally choosing their home
