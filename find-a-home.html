Before We Get Started — FAQ

Won't my agent find my perfect home?

Unlikely — with tools like these, you're really in the driver's seat in finding the homes you look at and the one you'll eventually purchase. A couple of reasons why: first, you'll search for homes on your coffee break, at lunch, while you're watching TV, or any other time you have a few minutes — your agent isn't searching that often on your behalf alone. Second, as you look at homes online and in person and see what's actually available, you make micro-adjustments — sometimes without even realizing it. Or you come to understand that the ideal home you had in your mind doesn't exist in real life, or not at your price — it only existed in your mind, and you adjust. Many people end up buying a home that doesn't quite match the picture they started with, and that's normal. Your agent can still set up an automatic search — see search criteria below — which can send you homes as soon as they hit the market, or on a regular batch schedule.

What am I paying an agent for, then?

Their skills in contract management and negotiation, tracking deadlines, managing the transaction, offering advice, and helping manage the emotions and emotional separation that come with one of the biggest purchases of your life.

Search Smart, Not Just Wide

You can do a lot of productive house-hunting before you leave your home. Homes.com, Zillow, Realtor.com and Redfin are all useful and provide different data in different formats, but each is primarily a marketing platform and a search tool second. Here's how to use them efficiently, how to keep from turning your search into a lead that gets sold to agents who make your phone ring incessantly, and how to narrow your search from your couch before you spend a minute visiting homes in person — saving you time and frustration.

Before You Search

Know Your Budget

Before you fall in love with a home based on how it looks online, get clear on what a comfortable monthly payment looks like. A lender can help you understand what you may qualify to borrow, but your financial comfort level matters just as much. Your total monthly housing cost includes more than the loan payment:

  • Principal and interest
  • Property taxes
  • Homeowners insurance
  • Mortgage insurance, if applicable
  • HOA or condominium fees
  • Flood insurance, if needed
  • Utility differences between homes
  • Expected maintenance and repair costs

A good goal is to keep money available beyond the down payment and closing costs. Homes will need repairs and maintenance, moving expenses, furniture, window treatments, tools, appliances, or utility deposits soon after closing.

Financing readiness checklist

  • Talk with a lender early and understand your loan options and programs
    i
    There are an amazing number of loan programs and options out there. Not all buyers qualify for all loan programs, and not all loans are suitable for all homes. A great lender will guide you through which ones actually apply to you.
  • Focus on the monthly payment estimate — this is what you're responsible for on a regular basis. The purchase price is a useful search criterion, but it's too big a number to realistically break down (and you might get genuinely depressed seeing the full amount paid after interest over the life of the loan)
  • Compare estimated monthly payments at different price points — know what your payment looks like in $20,000–$50,000 increments
  • Be sure to include taxes, insurance, HOA/condo fees, and mortgage insurance (if needed) to get the best picture of your monthly payment
  • Decide how much cash you want to keep after closing for repairs, moving, and emergencies
  • Avoid major financial changes while you're preparing to buy — new debt, large purchases, job changes, or unexplained account transfers. See Freeze Your Financial Picture for the full list.
Pro Tip
Getting a Pre-approval letter helps you search with clarity. You are not obligated to buy at the top of your approved range, nor are you obligated to use that lender when it comes to purchase. It's like a pre-check on your financial position, used to avoid future problems. Not sure what your budget looks like? Use my budget calculator before you start scrolling listings to get a good idea — but you'll still need to talk to a lender for a real letter. I can recommend some great ones.
Step 1

Search From the Comfort of Home

Start with all four major search sites. No one site is perfect or all-inclusive — the way a home is presented may look different on each one, and content updates at a different pace across each. This is the fun part.

Avoid clicking "Get More Info," "Contact Agent," "Request a Tour," "Ask a Question," or "I'm Interested" unless you are ready for your phone to blow up. Remember, these sites are primarily a marketing platform and a search tool second — they want your contact information to sell to agents who will hound you, forever. Instead: sites typically let you make an account (exercise caution), save the listing link or share it with a friend (or 804-Michael, if you're working with him), and keep your own notes. Set Your First Filters

Begin broad enough that you don't accidentally hide a good match, and don't use too many criteria to limit your options. You're also relying on agents or sellers entering data about a home correctly, and some make errors or omit information — don't let a lack of data unnecessarily limit your search.

Starting filters

  • Maximum purchase price or monthly payment (recommend adding about $10,000–$20,000 above your comfort price to catch homes that might be priced too high)
  • Minimum bedrooms and bathrooms
  • Your location, either based on proximity to important schools or work

Secondary refinement — if you're coming up with too many options, use these to narrow your search:

Secondary refinement

  • Minimum or maximum finished square footage
  • Specific school zone
  • Lot size, if outdoor space matters
  • Property type — single-family, townhouse, condo, manufactured
  • Parking needs — driveway, garage, number of spaces
  • Must-haves — first-floor bedroom, home office, basement, fenced yard, no HOA, public water/sewer, accessibility
  • Listing status — active first, then review pending and recently sold to understand competition and pricing
Note on Criteria. There are certain features that significantly limit home searches and should be used with caution, especially when combined: first-floor bedroom, garage, basement, and accessory dwelling unit (ADU).

Don't save 75 "favorites." Keep a focused list of 5–15 active possibilities, and revisit it often. Step 2

What to Look For in Every Listing

Before falling in love with the photos, review the listing details carefully.

Green Flags

  • Clear photos of the kitchen, bathrooms, bedrooms, yard, exterior, and mechanical areas
  • A floor plan or room dimensions
  • Specific ages or replacement dates for the roof, HVAC, windows, water heater, and appliances
  • Clear notes about utilities, HOA, parking, lot size, and included features
  • Listing history that makes sense
  • Exterior photos that show the home from multiple angles
  • Details about renovations, permits, maintenance, and improvements

Slow-Down Signals

  • Few or missing photos of bathrooms, basement, backyard, utility areas, or exterior
  • Photos that seem overly edited, heavily cropped, dark, or unusually wide-angle
  • "As-is," "cash only," "investor special," "needs TLC," "buyer to verify," or "seller makes no representations"
  • A home repeatedly returning to the market
  • Large price changes without an obvious explanation
  • Vague language such as "newer," "updated," or "recently replaced" without dates
  • Square footage that may combine finished and unfinished areas
  • A low price that seems out of line with nearby homes

None of these automatically means "walk away." They mean slow down, verify, and ask better questions.

Step 3

Explore the Area, Virtually

Once a home looks promising, open its address in Google Maps and switch between standard map view, satellite view, Street View, and directions. This helps you begin evaluating the property and its context, not merely the home's staged photos.

Satellite view checklist

  • Lot shape, driveway access, parking layout, turning space, and shared-driveway concerns
  • Distance to neighboring homes and backyard privacy
  • Whether the yard is flat, sloped, wooded, heavily shaded, or hard to maintain
  • Nearby commercial or industrial uses, rail lines, highways, power lines, retention ponds, or apartment complexes
  • Rear-lot neighbors, easements, drainage paths, visible water features
  • Pools, sheds, fences, decks, detached garages, and accessory buildings
  • Tree coverage close to the house, especially large limbs over the roof or driveway
  • Whether the property backs to woods, another neighborhood, a busy road, or undeveloped land
  • Adjacent fields or vacant parcels that could change in the future

Street View checklist

  • Road width, traffic patterns, on-street parking, sidewalks, curbs, streetlights
  • The condition and style of nearby homes
  • Visible drainage ditches, steep driveways, blind curves, or difficult entrances
  • Signs of neighborhood upkeep — yards, roofs, vehicles, exterior maintenance, vacant homes
  • Proximity to a fire station, school, church, shopping center, rail line, highway, or commercial property
  • How close the home sits to the road, and whether the front approach feels safe and practical
  • Mailboxes, utility poles, shared driveways, or access issues photos may not reveal
  • Nearby airport or flight-path considerations, where relevant

Street View imagery can be older than the listing, so treat it as a clue, not proof. Its real value is in spotting questions to investigate during the actual tour — use the clock icon to compare past years and see how the block has held up.

A Note about Well and Septic. Some rural properties have a private well, septic system, or both. These systems require some additional due diligence and inspection. Just because a home is marketed as a four-bedroom does not mean it has a septic system or well sized to support that many bedrooms.
Step 4

Check the Real-World Fit

A house can be attractive online and still be wrong for your daily life. Before scheduling a visit, check:

  • Commute — run directions to work, school, family, and frequent destinations during the times you'd normally travel, not Maps' default estimate.
  • Daily errands — grocery stores, pharmacies, fuel, restaurants, medical care, parks, and child-care needs.
  • Internet and cell service — especially important in rural or edge-of-service pockets of Hanover, Goochland, Powhatan, and Caroline counties.
  • Utilities — public water and sewer versus private well and septic; propane, oil, electric, or natural-gas heat; HOA fees and what they actually cover.
  • Flood and drainage — flood-risk resources, nearby creeks, low areas, drainage ditches, and the home's relationship to the surrounding terrain.
  • Schools — verify the current attendance zone and enrollment information directly with the applicable school division, not just a portal's estimate. See the Schools section of the Due Diligence guide for direct links.
  • Future change — vacant land, large fields, old commercial buildings, or road projects nearby that could change the immediate area.

Virginia Buyer Reminder: An Omission Is Not an All-Clear

In Virginia, the Residential Property Disclosure Act generally emphasizes buyer due diligence. The seller's residential property disclosure statement says owners make no representations or warranties about the property's condition or improvements, and it specifically advises buyers to obtain whatever due diligence they consider necessary — including inspections and other appropriate evaluations — during any inspection period that may be in the Purchase Agreement.

That means you shouldn't assume a seller has confirmed:

  • The overall condition of the house
  • Lot lines, setback rules, lot coverage, or whether an addition can be built
  • Adjacent parcels, zoning, or future uses next door
  • Flood risk or flood-insurance requirements, or dam-break inundation zones
  • Conservation easements or other easements
  • Septic-system type, size, condition, maintenance needs, or pump-out requirements
  • Radon risk, marine clay or soil concerns, or lead-pipe concerns
  • Airport or aircraft noise, or military-installation impacts
  • Historic-district or resource-protection-area restrictions
  • Registered sexual-offender information

Virginia's current disclosure statement also highlights items sellers must affirmatively disclose in writing, including pending building-code or zoning violations, lis pendens, prior methamphetamine manufacturing, privately owned stormwater-management facilities, repetitive-risk-loss status, and septic-system operating-permit validity.

A listing may not tell you everything you need to know — and under Virginia's disclosure framework, it may not have to. Ask questions, review documents, investigate the land and surrounding area, and use the appropriate contingencies and inspections before removing protections or moving forward to closing.

This is educational information, not legal advice. The exact disclosures and protections that apply can depend on the property, transaction, contract, and circumstances. Source: Virginia DPOR — Residential Property Disclosures.

Step 5

Build a Shortlist Worksheet

For each home, capture the same information so you can compare homes fairly:

Shortlist fields

  • Address and listing link
  • Asking price and estimated monthly payment
  • Beds, baths, finished square footage, lot size, and year built
  • Water, sewer, heating fuel, and internet notes
  • Top three advantages
  • Top three concerns
  • What needs verification
  • HOA amount and key restrictions
  • Whether you'd tour it — yes, no, or maybe
  • Your "gut score" from 1–10
